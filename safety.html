HLA genotyping

HLA-B*15:02 and severe cutaneous reactions

This allele carries a strong risk of Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis with carbamazepine. Test before starting carbamazepine in anyone with ancestry in populations where the allele occurs, and do not use carbamazepine in carriers unless the benefit clearly outweighs the risk. The same allele appears to raise risk with phenytoin, and caution extends to oxcarbazepine and lamotrigine.

Over 90% of carbamazepine-associated Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis occurs within the first few months of treatment.

HLA-A*31:01

Retrospective case-control studies in patients of European, Korean and Japanese ancestry found a moderate association between this allele and carbamazepine hypersensitivity, including Stevens-Johnson syndrome, maculopapular eruption and drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms. In Korea and Japan, where HLA-B*15:02 is present in under 1% of people, this is the allele that matters.

Class-wide effects

Suicidal ideation and behaviour

The United States regulator applies this warning to the class as a whole. Ask about mood at review, particularly with levetiracetam, perampanel and topiramate, and particularly in people with a psychiatric history.

Abrupt withdrawal

Stopping any antiseizure medication suddenly risks rebound seizures and status epilepticus. This applies to every agent in this formulary, including the one being withdrawn during a planned substitution.

Pregnancy

Ask every patient of childbearing potential about pregnancy plans at every review, not only at diagnosis. The drug chosen at the first consultation is often still in use years later when a pregnancy occurs.

Valproate

Beyond structural malformations, in-utero valproate exposure reduces IQ and raises the risk of neurodevelopmental disorders including autism. Around one in nine exposed babies has a birth defect and 30 to 40 in 100 have learning difficulties.

Under current United Kingdom regulatory rules, valproate must not be started in anyone of either sex under 55 unless two specialists independently document that no other treatment is effective and tolerated. Continuation requires two-specialist sign-off at the first annual review and one thereafter. Women and girls of childbearing potential require a Pregnancy Prevention Programme. Men should use contraception, because of a possible increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders in children conceived within three months of paternal exposure.

Practical points

  • Prescribe folic acid before conception and through the first trimester.
  • Keep monotherapy at the lowest effective dose. Polytherapy and higher doses both raise malformation risk.
  • Do not stop an effective drug abruptly on discovering a pregnancy. Organogenesis is largely complete by the time most pregnancies are confirmed, and convulsive seizures carry their own risk.
  • Monitor lamotrigine, levetiracetam and oxcarbazepine concentrations through pregnancy, raise the dose as levels fall, and plan the postpartum reduction in advance to avoid toxicity.

Contraception

  • Enzyme-inducing drugs — carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, primidone and topiramate — reduce hormonal contraceptive effectiveness. Choose a method that does not depend on hepatic metabolism, such as a copper or levonorgestrel intrauterine device.
  • Perampanel at 12 mg, rufinamide and cenobamate also reduce hormonal contraceptive reliability.
  • Estrogen-containing contraceptives and hormone replacement therapy roughly halve lamotrigine concentrations. The maintenance dose often needs doubling when one is started and halving when it is stopped, and levels rise transiently during the pill-free week.
  • Progestogen-only preparations do not appear to affect lamotrigine.
  • Levetiracetam, brivaracetam, lacosamide, gabapentin, pregabalin, valproate, vigabatrin and zonisamide do not reduce contraceptive effectiveness.

Stopping treatment

Consider discussing withdrawal once a person has been seizure-free for at least two years. The decision is individual and turns on syndrome, EEG, neuroimaging, seizure type, how hard control was to achieve, and what a recurrence would cost that person in driving, employment and confidence. Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, structural lesions and a persistently abnormal EEG all argue for continuing.

  • Withdraw one drug at a time, and only after the previous withdrawal is complete and stable.
  • Taper gradually over at least three months for most drugs, and over considerably longer for benzodiazepines and barbiturates.
  • Lamotrigine's label specifies at least two weeks, roughly 50% per week, when it must come off faster for safety reasons. For elective withdrawal after seizure freedom, go slower.
  • Clobazam is reduced by 5 to 10 mg per day each week; pause or step back if withdrawal symptoms appear.
  • If a seizure occurs during withdrawal, restore the last dose that maintained control and reassess.
  • Agree the driving implications in advance with the patient, according to local licensing rules.
