Therapeutic drug monitoring

Blood levels

A reference range is a population range, not a therapeutic target. Some patients do well below it and some need concentrations above it. Know the number when the clinical picture is ambiguous, then treat the patient.

Interpret a level

Places a measured concentration against the published range and reminds you what else governs its meaning.

When a level helps

Situations where the result changes management

  • Establishing an individual's own effective concentration once seizure-free, as a baseline for later comparison.
  • Suspected non-adherence, which is the commonest cause of apparent drug resistance.
  • Suspected toxicity when symptoms are hard to separate from the epilepsy itself.
  • Phenytoin at any time, because saturable kinetics mean a small dose rise can produce a large concentration rise.
  • Pregnancy, where lamotrigine, levetiracetam and oxcarbazepine concentrations fall substantially.
  • Renal or hepatic disease, hypoalbuminaemia, the very young and the elderly, where protein binding or clearance shifts.
  • Adding or removing an enzyme inducer or inhibitor.

Situations where it does not

A well-controlled patient with no adverse effects does not need a routine level. Neither does a patient on vigabatrin, whose effect is disconnected from its concentration, nor one on a drug with no established range: cenobamate, fenfluramine, ganaxolone and cannabidiol all post-date the 2018 review. Everolimus is the exception among the newer agents, and is titrated to a whole-blood trough of 5 to 15 ng/mL.

How to sample

  • Sample at steady state. The exceptions are overdose, suspected toxicity, and status epilepticus, where you sample immediately.
  • Take the sample immediately before the morning dose, and record the interval since the last dose so the result can be interpreted.
  • For drugs with a half-life under 12 hours, diurnal variation is large enough to change the interpretation. Timing matters most for levetiracetam, gabapentin, pregabalin, tiagabine, rufinamide and valproate.
  • Measure the free concentration rather than the total for phenytoin and valproate in hypoalbuminaemia, renal or hepatic disease, and pregnancy. Perampanel and stiripentol are also highly protein bound.
  • Monitor the active metabolite where one carries the effect: N-desmethylclobazam with clobazam, phenobarbital derived from primidone, the carbamazepine epoxide when an inhibitor is co-prescribed.
