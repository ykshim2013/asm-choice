Acute management

Status epilepticus

The single most common error in convulsive status epilepticus is under-dosing the benzodiazepine. Give an adequate single full dose rather than splitting it into several small ones.

The timed algorithm

Four phases. At each decision point, if the patient is back at baseline, move to symptomatic medical care instead.

PhaseTimingWhat to do
Stabilisation 0–5 min Airway, breathing, circulation, neurological examination. Time the seizure from onset. Oxygen; consider intubation. ECG monitoring. Finger-stick glucose — if below 60 mg/dL give thiamine 100 mg IV then 50 mL of 50% dextrose in adults, 2 mL/kg of 25% dextrose in children 2 years and over, 4 mL/kg of 12.5% dextrose under 2 years. IV access, electrolytes, haematology, toxicology, and antiseizure drug levels where relevant.
Initial therapy
benzodiazepine		 5–20 min A benzodiazepine is the initial therapy of choice (Level A). Choose one of three equivalent options at a full dose: intramuscular midazolam, intravenous lorazepam, or intravenous diazepam. If none is available, use intravenous phenobarbital 15 mg/kg (Level A), rectal diazepam (Level B), or intranasal or buccal midazolam (Level B).
Second therapy 20–40 min There is no evidence-based preferred agent (Level U). Choose one and give it as a single dose: fosphenytoin, valproic acid or levetiracetam intravenously. If none is available and it has not already been given, intravenous phenobarbital 15 mg/kg.
Third therapy 40–60 min No clear evidence guides this phase. Repeat second-line therapy, or move to anaesthetic doses of thiopental, midazolam, pentobarbital or propofol, all with continuous EEG monitoring.

Glauser T, Shinnar S, Gloss D, et al. Evidence-based guideline: treatment of convulsive status epilepticus in children and adults. Epilepsy Curr 2016;16(1):48–61. The ESETT trial subsequently found fosphenytoin, valproate and levetiracetam equivalent in benzodiazepine-refractory status, supporting the guideline's neutrality between them.

Home rescue medication

For seizure clusters treated outside hospital. Every patient given rescue medication needs a written seizure action plan naming the drug, the dose, when to give it, and when to call emergency services.

PreparationDoseRepeat and limits
Midazolam nasal spray One 5 mg spray into one nostril, aged 12 and over One further 5 mg spray into the other nostril after 10 minutes if there has been no response. Maximum 2 doses per episode, no more than one episode every 3 days and 5 episodes a month.
Diazepam nasal spray 0.5 mg/kg aged 2–5, 0.3 mg/kg aged 6–11, 0.2 mg/kg aged 12 and over, rounded to the 5, 10, 15 or 20 mg devices Second dose after at least 4 hours if needed. Maximum 2 doses per episode, one episode every 5 days, 5 episodes a month.
Diazepam rectal gel 0.2–0.5 mg/kg, maximum 20 mg A single dose within the acute algorithm; follow local product instructions for repeat dosing at home.

Before you prescribe a nasal benzodiazepine

For patients at increased risk of respiratory depression, consider giving the first dose under supervision, outside a seizure, so the caregiver has seen it done and the response is known.

After the seizure stops

  • Establish or re-establish maintenance therapy before the loading dose wears off. A patient loaded with fosphenytoin or levetiracetam and then sent home without maintenance will seize again.
  • Look for the precipitant: missed doses, a new interacting drug, infection, sleep deprivation, alcohol, metabolic derangement, or a new structural lesion.
  • Check concentrations where the drug has a useful reference range, particularly phenytoin, whose kinetics are saturable.
  • In a patient not previously known to have epilepsy, the cause of the status is the diagnostic question, not the seizure itself.
  • Review whether home rescue medication and a written action plan are in place before discharge.
