Pharmacology

Where the drugs act

Every marketed antiseizure medication shifts the balance between excitation and inhibition at one of a small number of molecular targets. Knowing which target a drug occupies is what makes a rational combination possible, and explains why some agents are narrow-spectrum and others broad.

Targets by class

Sills and Rogawski group antiseizure mechanisms into four broad classes, with a fifth for mechanism-targeted agents.

ClassTargetAgentsClinical signature
Sodium channel Voltage-gated NaV channels, inactivated state; some also block the persistent current Phenytoin, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, eslicarbazepine, lamotrigine, lacosamide, rufinamide, cenobamate Effective in focal seizures. Dose-related diplopia, ataxia and dizziness. Several worsen absence and myoclonic seizures, and all are contraindicated in Dravet syndrome.
Calcium channel T-type CaV3 in thalamus; alpha-2-delta-1 auxiliary subunit presynaptically Ethosuximide, zonisamide and valproate at T-type; gabapentin and pregabalin at alpha-2-delta-1 T-type blockade is specific to absence seizures. The gabapentinoids are narrow-spectrum add-ons that cause weight gain and oedema.
GABA enhancement GABA-A receptor as a positive allosteric modulator, or GABA disposition through GABA-transaminase and GAT-1 Benzodiazepines, phenobarbital, primidone, stiripentol, ganaxolone, cenobamate; vigabatrin and tiagabine on disposition Broad efficacy but sedation, tolerance and dependence. Vigabatrin carries irreversible visual field loss; tiagabine can precipitate non-convulsive status.
Glutamate blockade AMPA receptor, non-competitively; NMDA receptor for felbamate Perampanel; felbamate; topiramate in part A narrow therapeutic window, because AMPA receptors carry most fast excitatory transmission. Serious behavioural reactions are the limiting effect of perampanel.
Vesicle protein SV2A, a presynaptic vesicle protein whose physiological role remains unclear Levetiracetam, brivaracetam Broad efficacy, very few interactions, rapid titration. Behavioural adverse effects are the usual reason for stopping.
Mechanism-targeted The pathogenic defect itself Everolimus for mTOR pathway activation in tuberous sclerosis complex The first genuinely disease-specific antiseizure therapy. There is no evidence it works in epilepsies other than those associated with tuberous sclerosis.

Building a combination

After two appropriate monotherapy failures, combination therapy is reasonable. The conventional advice is to combine drugs with different mechanisms, and although the experimental evidence for mechanistic synergy has largely failed to translate, two clinical observations support the practice.

  • Valproate with lamotrigine is the best-accepted combination, with an apparently favourable interaction that is both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic. The same pairing carries the highest risk of serious rash, so use the valproate-specific lamotrigine titration.
  • Stacking sodium-channel blockers works less well. In the lacosamide registration data, adding lacosamide to a regimen that already contained a sodium-channel blocker produced less benefit and more adverse effects than adding it to a regimen without one.
  • Some combinations add nothing but toxicity. Levetiracetam with brivaracetam, gabapentin with pregabalin, and oxcarbazepine with eslicarbazepine each pair two agents acting at the same target. The last of these delivers the identical active molecule twice.
  • A single drug can be its own combination. Cenobamate acts on the persistent sodium current and on GABA-A receptors at clinical concentrations, and topiramate, zonisamide and felbamate each act at several targets. This polypharmacology may be why some of them outperform their single-mechanism relatives.

What we still do not know

Sills and Rogawski are blunt about the limits. Antiseizure drugs prevent seizures; none has been shown to modify the disease. In most epilepsies the drugs do not address the pathogenic defect, because that defect is not known. Valproate has been in use for more than fifty years and there is still debate about which of its many cellular effects produces the clinical benefit. Levetiracetam's target was identified only after it was licensed, and what binding to SV2A actually does remains unresolved.

More than thirty molecular entities have entered practice since phenytoin, and the proportion of patients achieving seizure freedom has not improved. That is the strongest argument for spending effort on classification, adherence and surgical candidacy rather than on the next drug in the sequence.
